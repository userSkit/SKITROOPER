# The ten classics to launch with — ranked, with the reasoning

Written 2026-09-11. Sources and method at the bottom. Every number marked **(confirm)** needs a
two-minute check in a live calculator before you price anything. Nothing here has been spent.

## How the ranking was actually done

Most "best classic books" lists rank by reputation. Reputation does not pay for a print run. Each
title was scored out of 5 on four things that decide whether it makes money, and the four were
weighted.

| Factor | Weight | What it measures |
|---|---|---|
| Demand | ×3 | Is anyone actually looking for this book, right now, in a form you could sell? |
| Margin | ×2 | Page count. Page count is the whole cost story in print-on-demand (see below). |
| Shelf appeal | ×2 | Would someone buy this as an object, for a shelf, as a gift, without reading it? |
| Rights safety | ×3 | Can you print this text, in this translation, without a lawyer? |

Rights safety is weighted as heavily as demand deliberately. It is the one factor on this list that
can cost you the business rather than cost you a sale.

### The page-count insight, which is the one that matters

In print-on-demand you do not pay per book. You pay a binding charge plus a charge per page. The
working model, from the published Lulu figures, is roughly:

```
print cost ≈ €7.50 (hardcover binding) + €0.016 × page count
```

That means *The Art of War* costs €9.26 to make and *Jane Eyre* costs €15.82. Sold at the same
price, the short book earns €6.56 more per copy than the long one, for identical work. Over a
hundred sales that is €656 — more than four times the entire launch budget.

So the list is deliberately weighted toward short, famous books. Six of the ten are under 300 pages.
*Jane Eyre* is ranked last not because it sells badly, but because it is 520 pages and every copy
sold earns a third less than a copy of *Meditations*. Include it because people ask for it; do not
build the marketing around it.

### The rights trap, which is the one that gets people

The original texts are all long out of copyright. **Translations are not.** A translation is a new
copyrighted work, and its clock runs from the translator's death, not the author's. In the EU that
is life plus seventy years.

This is the single most expensive mistake available in this business, so each Homer edition is
named explicitly:

| Translation | Translator died | Status | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Samuel Butler (1898–1900, prose) | 1902 | Public domain worldwide | **Use this one** |
| Alexander Pope (1720s, verse) | 1744 | Public domain worldwide | Safe, but the couplets are hard going |
| Butcher & Lang / Lang, Leaf & Myers | 1910 / 1912 | Public domain worldwide | Safe alternative |
| George Chapman (1616) | 1634 | Public domain worldwide | Archaic; a curiosity |
| Robert Fitzgerald (1961) | 1985 | In copyright until 2056 | **Do not touch** |
| Richmond Lattimore (1951) | 1984 | In copyright until 2055 | **Do not touch** |
| Robert Fagles (1996) | 2008 | In copyright until 2079 | **Do not touch** |
| Emily Wilson (2017) | living | In copyright | **Do not touch** |

The same rule applies to *Meditations* (use George Long, died 1879), *The Art of War* (use Lionel
Giles, died 1958 — public domain in the EU from 2029, **so use the 1910 text as published in the US,
where it is public domain; see the note below**) and *Crime and Punishment* (Constance Garnett, died
1946, public domain in the EU since 2017).

> **Open item on The Art of War.** Giles died in 1958, which puts his translation in copyright in the
> EU until 1 January 2029 under life-plus-seventy, even though the 1910 text is public domain in the
> United States. Two clean ways out: sell that title to US customers only until 2029, or use the 1910
> Giles text only if a rights check confirms it was never renewed and is therefore free in the EU as a
> pre-1996 US public-domain work. Until that is settled, **launch without The Art of War** and put
> *Wuthering Heights* in the tenth slot. This is flagged rather than guessed at, because guessing is
> how you get a takedown.

Titles written originally in English — Austen, Shelley, Wilde, Stoker, Brontë, Fitzgerald — have no
translation risk at all. That is a quiet argument for leaning on them first.

### Where the text files come from

- **Standard Ebooks** — public-domain classics, professionally typeset and proofread, released into
  the public domain under CC0. Explicitly free for commercial use. This is the best source and it is
  the one to use.
- **Project Gutenberg** — the texts themselves are public domain. The *Project Gutenberg* name is a
  trademark and their licence attaches only if you keep their header and branding. Strip the header
  and the trademark, and the underlying text is yours to set freely.
- **Wikisource** — public domain, less consistently proofread.

Never copy a modern paperback. The text, the introduction, the notes and the typographic setting are
all somebody's property.

## Title-by-title, briefly

**1. Meditations.** A Roman emperor's private notebook. The stoicism revival has been running for a
decade and shows no sign of stopping; this is the book that revival points at. 160 pages of short
numbered passages, which also means it looks good open on a table. Long's 1862 translation is
austere and out of copyright.

**2. The Odyssey.** The one you asked for, and it earns the place. It is the most recognisable title
in the collection, it is the one that makes a shelf look serious, and Butler's prose translation is
readable in a way Pope's verse is not. At 330 pages it sits comfortably in the middle of the cost
range.

**3. The Art of War.** 110 pages. Nothing else on the list is close. Sold at the same price as a
450-page *Dracula* it earns €5.44 more per copy. Corporate and father's-day gifting is real and
year-round. Held back only by the Giles rights question above.

**4. The Picture of Dorian Gray.** Wilde is Dublin-born, which matters if the press is Irish-run: it
gives you a true story to tell rather than a marketing angle. The book is also the visual centre of
the dark-academia aesthetic, which is the audience this brand is built for.

**5. Frankenstein.** Permanently on school and university reading lists, which means permanent
baseline demand that owes nothing to trends. Written by a nineteen-year-old woman in 1816, which is
a better story than most publishers manage.

**6. The Great Gatsby.** 200 pages, universally recognised, and the copyright question is finally
settled: public domain in the EU since 2011 (Fitzgerald died 1940) and in the US since 2021.
Short book, famous title, no translator — one of the best margin-to-demand ratios available.

**7. Pride and Prejudice.** The best-selling classic novel in English by a wide margin, and the one
most often bought as a gift by someone who is not the reader. 400 pages hurts the margin. Sell it in
sets rather than alone.

**8. Dracula.** Stoker was born in Clontarf. The second Irish title, and the one with a built-in
seasonal spike every October that you can plan content around months ahead. 450 pages is expensive;
price accordingly or keep it to sets.

**9. The Iliad.** Honestly, this is here for structural reasons rather than its own demand. Nobody
buys one half of Homer. Stocking both turns a €42 sale into a €108 sale, and that is the entire
argument for it.

**10. Jane Eyre.** Consistently in the top five most-downloaded books on every public-domain
tracker, so the demand is not in doubt. 520 pages makes it the thinnest margin here. Carry it, do
not lead with it.

## What this list is not

It is not a prediction that these ten will sell. It is a ranked shortlist built on demand signals,
cost arithmetic and rights safety, which is as far as desk research can honestly take you. The list
gets tested the moment real money moves, and the test is cheap: print-on-demand means an unsold
title costs nothing but the hour spent designing its cover.

## Method and sources

Demand was triangulated across four independent signals rather than a single bestseller list,
because no single list covers this category well:

1. **Public-domain download rankings** — Project Gutenberg publishes running download counts, which
   are the closest thing that exists to a demand index for out-of-copyright titles specifically.
   Direct access to the ranking page was blocked from this environment; figures came through search
   results and third-party mirrors of the list, so the ordering is directional and should be
   re-checked against [the live Top 100](https://www.gutenberg.org/browse/scores/top).
2. **Retail bestseller positions** in Amazon's Classic Literature and Classic American Literature
   categories.
3. **Curriculum presence** — titles that sit on school and university reading lists have demand that
   does not depend on fashion.
4. **Aesthetic-market demand** — the dark-academia subculture, which is where premium classic
   editions are actually bought as objects. It is documented as a durable rather than passing trend,
   with millions of posts across Pinterest, Instagram and TikTok.

Rights status was checked against translation dates and translator death dates under the EU
life-plus-seventy rule and the US pre-1930 publication rule.
