The Press

Lampblack is the soot a lamp leaves behind.

It is also what the best printing ink was made of for four hundred years. Collected off the inside of a chimney, ground into oil, and pressed into paper. We took the name because it is the least glamorous part of a beautiful process, and that is roughly the right attitude.

Why

Great books, badly made.

The best writing in the language is now free. Copyright has run out on all of it, which should have been wonderful news, and in one way it was: you can read every book we publish tonight, legally, for nothing.

What happened instead is that free text got treated like worthless text. Walk into any shop and the classics are the cheapest thing on the shelf, printed on grey paper in a face nobody chose, with a stock photograph on the cover and a typeface stretched to fill the space. The message is unmistakable, and it is wrong.

A book that survived two hundred years of readers deciding whether to keep it deserves to be made like it was worth the wait.

That is the entire idea. There is no manifesto after this paragraph.

How

One copy at a time, and we will not pretend otherwise.

Your book is printed after you order it, at a press inside the European Union, and posted directly to you. We hold no stock. We have never pulped a book and we never will, because we have never printed one that somebody had not already asked for.

Plenty of publishers do this and would rather you did not know. We would rather say it, for two reasons. It is why the book takes three to five working days before it ships, and you deserve to know that before you order rather than after. And it is why the money goes into the cloth, the stamping and the typesetting instead of into a warehouse full of books nobody bought.

What you get is a cased hardback, roughly fourteen by twenty-one centimetres, cloth-look board, gold on the front and the spine, printed on cream uncoated stock. It is not a hand-bound antiquarian volume and we will never describe it as one.

The rules we set ourselves

Four of them, and they are not negotiable.

01

Name the translation

Every title page says which edition the text came from and what year it dates to. If you cannot tell whose Homer you are buying, somebody is hoping you will not ask.

02

Use only what is free and clear

Public-domain source texts, and translations whose copyright has genuinely expired. Never a modern translation reprinted without permission, whatever it would save.

03

Set it again, properly

The text is typeset from scratch, not photographed from a paperback. That is the difference between a facsimile and an edition.

04

Never oversell the object

No leather it does not have, no gilt edges it does not have, no hand-binding that did not happen. The book in the picture is the book in the parcel.

Who

One person, in Dublin.

Not a house, not an imprint of anything larger. Trading details are in the footer, as Irish consumer law requires, and the email address goes to a person rather than a ticketing system.
